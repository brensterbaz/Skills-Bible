# Script to Storyboard

Turn one spoken-word script into a clear scene plan without silently rewriting the words or pretending the finished visual treatment already exists.

## Establish the input

Identify:

- whether the script is approved or still a working draft;
- its audience, intended format, and approximate length;
- whether the wording must remain verbatim;
- any claims, examples, or calls to action that need special care.

When approval status is unknown, treat the script as a working draft and say so. Preserve the supplied wording by default. If a line creates a structural problem, flag it for review instead of rewriting it without permission.

## Find the story spine

Read the whole script before dividing it. State the central movement in one sentence: what the audience understands at the start, and what they should understand or do by the end.

Split the script where the narrative job or viewer understanding changes. Do not make one scene per sentence, divide by equal word counts, or force a fixed number of scenes. Combine neighbouring lines when they serve one purpose; separate a short line when it creates a meaningful turn.

Useful narrative jobs include:

- hook or situation;
- problem or tension;
- reframe;
- explanation or mechanism;
- example;
- boundary or caveat;
- payoff;
- call to action.

## Build the scene map

For each scene, return:

1. **Scene and title** — a short label that helps people discuss the plan;
2. **Narrative job** — why the scene exists;
3. **Voiceover** — the exact line or lines assigned to it;
4. **Viewer understanding** — the one idea that should land;
5. **On-screen subject** — what the frame needs to communicate at a content level;
6. **Source or claim note** — evidence, wording, or approval that needs checking.

Keep the on-screen subject concrete but style-neutral. Describe the information or object that belongs on screen, not its colour palette, composition, camera move, animation, transition, typography, or production technique. Those decisions belong to visual direction.

Treat any duration as an estimate unless recorded audio or a timed read exists. Do not invent assets, evidence, product behaviour, statistics, or customer outcomes.

## Check continuity

Before returning the storyboard, verify that:

- every voiceover line appears exactly once;
- scenes follow a clear cause-and-effect or teaching sequence;
- each scene has one primary job;
- examples, boundaries, and the call to action have enough space to land;
- no storyboard note introduces a claim absent from the script or its sources.

## Deliver

Return:

1. **Assumptions** — only material assumptions;
2. **Story spine** — one sentence;
3. **Storyboard** — the ordered scene map;
4. **Continuity notes** — coverage, unresolved claims, and anything requiring human approval.

Stop at the narrative storyboard. Do not create visual direction, artwork, shot styling, audio, animation, or rendered media unless the user separately asks for that work.
