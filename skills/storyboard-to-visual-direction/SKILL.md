# Storyboard to Visual Direction

Give an approved storyboard a point of view that a designer or production agent can act on without mistaking fashionable decoration for a visual idea.

This skill creates the **visual-design brief**, not the final image or video generation prompt. Its output should tell the next person or skill what the sequence should look and feel like; a later production step translates that approved direction into model-specific prompts and assets.

## Establish the constraints

Identify:

- target format, aspect ratio, and viewing context;
- audience and desired emotional tone;
- brand colours, type, imagery, and accessibility requirements;
- real assets that must or must not appear;
- whether the storyboard and voiceover are approved.

If the format materially affects composition and is missing, state a working assumption. Do not silently rewrite the storyboard. Flag story problems separately from visual decisions.

## Find the visual point of view

Read the whole storyboard and name the human situation underneath it. Write one concrete direction sentence that joins subject, mood, and visual behaviour.

Prefer details that belong to this story: a real setting, an ordinary object, a physical texture, a familiar action, or a meaningful constraint. Reject generic technology shorthand unless the subject genuinely requires it. In particular, do not default to glowing brains, robot heads, purple-blue gradients, floating glass panels, identical cards, or decorative data streams.

## Define one visual system

Set the shared rules before directing individual scenes:

- **colour roles** — background, readable text, focal accent, warning or boundary;
- **typography roles** — display, body, labels, captions;
- **material and texture** — what makes the world feel lived-in rather than synthetic;
- **composition rhythm** — where density and breathing room change across the story;
- **graphic language** — diagrams, marks, labels, frames, or recurring objects;
- **motion character** — the kind of physical change that supports meaning;
- **caption and safe-area rules** — readability, contrast, and placement.

Use semantic roles rather than collecting fashionable colours or effects. Make important states understandable without colour alone.

## Direct each scene

For every storyboard scene, return:

1. **Visual intention** — what the viewer should feel or notice;
2. **Focal subject** — the one element that carries the scene;
3. **Composition** — hierarchy, placement, scale, and negative space;
4. **Supporting detail** — only details that strengthen the story;
5. **Reveal or motion cue** — a purposeful change, not decorative movement;
6. **Continuity link** — what visually carries into or out of the scene;
7. **Avoid** — the tempting but misleading or generic treatment to reject;
8. **Accessibility note** — captions, contrast, legibility, or non-colour cue.

Do not invent unavailable assets, customer evidence, product behaviour, or production results. Distinguish a creative direction from a photographed, illustrated, animated, or tested result.

## Keep the hand-off clear

Stay at the level of visual design and creative direction. Do not turn the scene treatments into final generation prompts, negative prompts, model syntax, seeds, rendering parameters, or tool-specific instructions.

When a later prompt-writing step would need information that is not approved, list it as an open production decision rather than filling it in. The approved output from this skill becomes source material for that later step.

## Check the whole sequence

Before delivering, confirm that:

- the visual system belongs to the subject rather than a generic AI aesthetic;
- scene layouts vary with narrative importance instead of repeating one template;
- the focal subject is obvious at mobile size;
- visual choices support the voiceover without duplicating every word;
- boundaries and caveats remain legible;
- captions and essential information have sufficient contrast and safe space;
- the final scene feels like a hand-off, not a decorative end card.

## Deliver

Return:

1. **Assumptions** — only material assumptions;
2. **Direction statement** — one concrete sentence;
3. **Visual system** — shared rules for the sequence;
4. **Scene direction** — the ordered scene-by-scene treatment;
5. **Production hand-off** — assets, claims, prompt decisions, or other production details still requiring approval.

Stop at visual direction. Do not write the final image or video generation prompt, create artwork, source media, generate audio, animate scenes, or render a video unless the user separately asks for that later production work.
